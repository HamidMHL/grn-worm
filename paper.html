Evolving a multicellular worm from a DNA-encoded gene regulatory network: a growth-limiting signal emerges, effective predator avoidance does not

Fable 5.21 and Hamid Mohamadlou2

1Claude AI model, Anthropic, working in Claude Code  ·  2Independent researcher

Preprint, 10 November 2026; revised 11 November 2026 (Section 3.6 replicated in 32 worlds). This manuscript has not been peer reviewed.

Abstract. We ask whether development, physiology, behaviour and reproduction can be encoded together in one small genome and recovered by evolution in a form simple enough to dissect gene by gene. Each simulated worm grows from a single egg cell into a chain of cells, all running the same gene regulatory network, which is read from a DNA string over four letters and mutated by substitutions, indels, and segmental duplications and deletions. A scored warm-up of 450 generations was followed by 200,000 ticks in a shared world with no fitness function, where lineages spread only by laying eggs that survive. The resulting population was self-sustaining (77 to 95 animals, 1.2 to 1.4 eggs per life, no immigration). Knockout and sense-blocking assays showed that the worms depend on a diffusible product, S1, that represses cell division: removing it raised body size from 4.2 to 6.9 cells and cut egg output by 79%. A maternal head marker whose half-life was shortened 25-fold during evolution became dispensable. Effective predator avoidance did not evolve, although predators caused about one third of deaths. Competition assays showed that a hand-wired flee reflex spreads to fixation when it triggers at close range and is eliminated when it triggers at long range. In 32 replicate worlds evolved for 400,000 further ticks under fourfold predator density, the worms used their enemy senses only weakly (blocking them raised the predation share of deaths by 4.7 points, standard error 0.8), 3 worlds remained self-sustaining, and none approached the hand-wired reflex. We report in full the six points at which evolution stalled and the rule changes that resolved them; in each case the population had responded correctly to the rules as written. All results derive from a single founding lineage in a deliberately minimal model.

Keywords: gene regulatory network; artificial development; artificial life; evolvability; body size control; morphogen.

1. Introduction

Every cell of an animal carries the same DNA. Cells differ because they express different genes, and expression is controlled by the products of other genes. This control system, the gene regulatory network (GRN), is what turns one genome into many cell types arranged in a body [1, 2]. A long-standing question in artificial life is how much of that process can be reproduced in a model small enough to be understood completely.

GRNs have been modelled as networks of continuous units with weighted activating and repressing inputs since the connectionist model of Mjolsness, Sharp and Reinitz [3]. Artificial embryogeny grows multicellular bodies from compact genomes [4, 5, 6]; evolved virtual creatures are scored on behaviour [7]; and neural cellular automata grow a target form from one cell under a shared local rule [8]. We make no claim of novelty for any component.

What we contribute is a single small system in which one DNA-encoded network controls growth, cell specialisation, feeding, excretion, locomotion and reproduction, followed by a genetic dissection of what evolved and a full account of where evolution failed. There is no formula that maps a target organism to a genome, so genomes were found by mutation and selection, and the result was then analysed with knockouts, as a geneticist would analyse a mutant.

2. Methods

2.1 Genome encoding

The genome is a string over A, C, G and T, about 920 letters long at initialisation. A gene begins at the promoter TATA. The next three letters specify its product, two letters its basal level, and one letter its rate constant. Each following six-letter block is a binding site: one continuation letter, three letters naming a regulator and two letters giving a signed strength between −4.5 and +4.5. A block beginning with T terminates the gene. Sequence between genes is non-coding. At most 64 genes are read.

2.2 Regulation

Each gene has a level in [0, 1] in each cell. At every tick the level relaxes, at the gene's rate, toward a logistic function of its basal term plus the weighted sum of its regulators. Regulators are the summed levels of the 12 transcription factor kinds, the local concentrations of the 3 signals, and 9 senses: food smell, lateral difference in food smell, enemy smell, lateral difference in enemy smell, body energy, gut food, gut waste, the maternal marker, and an edge sense that is 1 in the two end cells of the body.

2.3 Cell and body

A worm is a chain of up to 24 cells. The five physiological workers of a cell share a fixed capacity, so no cell can perform every function at full strength. Food enters at any cell expressing mouth, flows posteriorly through the gut, and is digested into energy and waste; waste must be expelled or it blocks the gut and becomes toxic. Bilateral muscle contraction propels the worm and a left–right difference turns it. A cell divides when its divide product exceeds 0.5 and body energy permits; the daughter is placed posterior to the mother with half her levels. The egg carries a maternal marker that remains in the anterior cell and decays exponentially.

2.4 World

The world is a 500 × 500 torus with food pellets that regrow in patches, and six predators that pursue worms within a detection radius that increases with body size. A bite on the head is lethal; a bite elsewhere removes that cell and all cells posterior to it.

2.5 Reproduction and mutation

A worm of at least five cells with sufficient energy lays an egg when its lay product is on. Offspring DNA is copied with substitutions (0.002 per letter), insertions or deletions of one to three letters (probability 0.08 per egg), and duplication (0.04) or deletion (0.015) of a 30 to 200 letter segment. An egg hatches only if its DNA retains at least one gene for each of the seven workers, and hatching probability falls with population density.

2.6 Evolution procedure

Stage 1 (scored). A population of 300 genomes, each initialised with one gene per product and random regulation, was evolved for 450 generations. Each genome was grown alone from an egg in six random worlds for 3,000 ticks and scored as energy assimilated plus 100 per egg laid. The best 15 genomes were retained and the remainder replaced by mutated copies of tournament winners, at three times the stage 2 substitution rate. The half-life of the maternal marker was held at 3,500 ticks until generation 135 and then shortened geometrically to 138 ticks by generation 337.

Stage 2 (unscored). The 40 best stage 1 genomes were released, two copies each, into four independent shared worlds for 200,000 ticks. No fitness is computed. A world that falls below 20 worms receives eggs from outside; this did not occur in the reported world.

2.7 Assays

For each perturbation, a fresh world was stocked with the final genomes with mutation disabled, one sense was clamped to zero or one product was forced to zero in every worm, and the world was run for 20,000 ticks. The primary measure is eggs laid per completed life. For competition assays, half of the founding worms and all of their descendants carried a hard-wired reflex that turns the head away from a predator whenever enemy smell exceeds a threshold; the reflex share of the population was recorded after 80,000 ticks.

3. Results

3.1 Bodies evolved first, then reproduction

Multicellular bodies appeared within 30 generations of stage 1, and egg laying became established between generations 100 and 125 (Fig. 1). Shortening the maternal marker's half-life 25-fold cost about half of egg output at its worst, near generation 300, after which the population recovered with the marker at its final setting.

3.2 A self-sustaining population

In stage 2 the population persisted for the full 200,000 ticks without immigration (Table 2). Of deaths in the sampled windows, 37% were from old age, 34% from starvation and 29% from predation. The deepest lineage at the end spanned 76 generations.

3.3 Dependence on senses and gene products

Egg output collapsed when the food, gut or energy senses were blocked, or when S1, T1, anus or divide was removed (Fig. 2). Without S1, worms grew to 6.9 cells instead of 4.2 and 70% starved. Without the energy sense they remained at 1.8 cells and never laid. Eleven of the twelve transcription factor kinds could be removed singly with no measurable effect (1.11 to 1.21 eggs per life). Blocking the maternal marker, the edge sense, or the enemy senses had no measurable effect on egg output. With enemy senses blocked, predators caused 34% of deaths against 32% in the unperturbed world, a difference that one world per condition cannot resolve; the replicated test in Section 3.6 shows that the enemy senses do have a small effect on predation.

3.5 Genome size

Initial genomes had 22 genes in about 920 letters. The stage 1 founders averaged 11.7 genes in 1,581 letters and the final population 10.4 genes in 1,611 letters: unused regulator genes were lost while non-coding sequence grew. Every final genome retained T1 and S1; 65% carried an S3 gene although its removal had no measurable effect.

3.6 Predator avoidance is favoured but evolved only weakly

When every worm was given the flee reflex with a threshold reached about 58 units from a predator, deaths from predation fell from 33% to 0%, yet eggs per life were unchanged (1.16 in both cases). In a saturated world, egg output per life is held near replacement whatever the worms do, so it is a poor measure of individual advantage, and we turned to competition.

In head-to-head competition (Table 4), the long-range reflex was eliminated or reduced in all eight worlds, most plausibly because carriers turn away from distant predators instead of feeding. Reflexes triggering only at close range rose from 50% to between 78% and 100% in all 16 worlds.

We then let the final population evolve for a further 400,000 ticks with 24 predators. In this first world (seed 777) it did not adapt: predation accounted for 83% to 87% of deaths throughout, the population fell to the immigration floor of about 22 worms, and about 300 eggs per 50,000 ticks were added from outside. Assayed afterwards, these worms did no better than their ancestors (82% of deaths from predation in both), and blocking their enemy senses made no clear difference (84% against 82%).

Because that conclusion rested on one world, we repeated the experiment in 32 worlds that differ only in random seed (seeds 1001 to 1032; exp_enemy_seeds.js), and assayed each resulting population in two worlds of 20,000 ticks with and without its enemy senses (Table 5). The replication changes the conclusion in two ways. First, the enemy senses are used, weakly: blocking them raised the predation share of deaths by 4.7 points on average (standard deviation 4.4, standard error 0.8), and by 5 points or more in 14 of the 32 populations. The same was true of the ancestors (82% against 87% when blocked), so this weak dependence predates the high-predation runs and the single-world assays above were too noisy to detect it. Second, the outcome varied between worlds. Three of the 32 remained self-sustaining over the last 50,000 ticks, with 71 to 82 worms and no eggs added, and in the two best the predation share fell to 70% and 74%, rising to 81% and 84% with the senses blocked. The other 29 fell to the immigration floor. Averaged over all 32, the evolved worms did no better than their ancestors (81% against 82%), and none approached the hand-wired close-range reflex.

4. Discussion

4.1 A self-produced growth inhibitor

The clearest result is that selection for reproduction in a food-limited world produced a body-size control circuit of a familiar kind. Division is driven by energy and shut off by a slowly accumulating, self-produced, diffusible inhibitor. Removing the inhibitor does not merely enlarge the animal; it makes it unviable, because a larger body costs more than it can gather. The model was not given any notion of target size.

4.2 Why avoidance may be hard to reach

The following is a hypothesis. The useful behaviour is conditional: turn away only when the enemy is close, and let that override the turn toward food. In this model a gene sums its inputs, so the simplest wiring, a lateral enemy sense acting directly on a muscle gene, yields the always-on reflex that competition shows to be harmful. The beneficial form needs a second input to gate the first, and there may be no path of single mutations that improves at every step. Consistent with this, every genome from the first high-predation run carried at least one enemy-sense binding site, and one inspected genome had the lateral enemy sense activating the correct muscle. The 32-world replication qualifies the hypothesis: weak avoidance is reachable and is occasionally improved, so the barrier, if there is one, lies between weak use of the sense and the strong, range-gated reflex. We have not measured how many mutations separate the evolved genomes from a genome that encodes that reflex; constructing one by hand and counting is the direct test.

4.3 Where evolution stalled

The reported run was the eighth attempt. Each earlier attempt stalled for an identifiable reason (Table 6), and each remedy is a design decision by the authors and not an evolved property. In every case the population had found a correct response to the rules as written. The energy-budget stall was measured directly by forcing a generation-190 worm to fixed sizes under the earlier costs (Table 7): evolution had stopped at the size the world could support.

4.4 Limitations

All results come from one stage 1 run with one random seed and one of four stage 2 worlds; whether the S1 circuit would evolve again is untested. The 32 high-predation worlds of Section 3.6 all start from that same evolved population, so they replicate the world and not the lineage. The rules in Table 6 shaped the outcome and other choices could yield a different animal. The body is a one-dimensional chain without mechanics, so nothing here bears on morphogenesis. The evolved network is shallow, with about 10 genes and one essential transcription factor. Behaviour is reflexive, with no nervous system and no learning. Reproduction is asexual. The edge sense was always available, and blocking it together with the maternal marker was not tested. Except in the 32-world experiment, each assay condition was run once, so differences of a few percent are within noise; the collapses to near zero are not. An earlier version of this manuscript concluded from such single runs that the worms ignore predators, which the replication showed to be too strong.

5. Conclusion

A genome of about ten genes, written in a four-letter code and shaped only by mutation and differential reproduction, was sufficient to grow, feed, excrete and reproduce a simple multicellular animal, and it arrived at an inhibitor-based mechanism for limiting its own size. The same process produced only weak predator avoidance: across 32 replicate worlds it never found the close-range reflex that demonstrably pays, which we attribute, provisionally, to the difficulty of reaching conditional behaviour incrementally in a network whose genes sum their inputs. Testing that explanation, by allowing binding sites that require two regulators together, is the natural next experiment.
